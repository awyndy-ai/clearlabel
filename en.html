EU AI Act Article 50 — quick answers

What is Article 50 of the EU AI Act?

Article 50 sets out the transparency obligations of the EU AI Act. It requires that people are told when they are interacting with an AI system (e.g. a chatbot), that AI-generated or AI-manipulated synthetic content is marked as such, that deepfakes are disclosed, and that AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest is labelled, unless a human took editorial responsibility.

When does Article 50 start to apply?

The Article 50 transparency obligations apply from 2 August 2026 (per Article 113). Separately, the Digital Omnibus — on which the European Parliament and Council reached a provisional agreement on 7 May 2026 — would give generative AI systems already on the market before 2 August 2026 until 2 December 2026 to meet the machine-readable marking requirement in Article 50(2). That extension is not yet adopted (still subject to formal endorsement and publication in the Official Journal), so treat 2 Dec 2026 as expected but not final, and re-check before relying on it.

Does Article 50 apply to my small business?

If your product or service reaches people in the EU and you run a chatbot, generate marketing content with AI, or publish AI-generated media, Article 50 likely applies regardless of company size. It is not limited to "high-risk" AI. The free check above shows which specific obligations are triggered by what you actually do.

What is the difference between a "provider" and a "deployer"?

A provider develops or places an AI system on the market (e.g. you build a chatbot). A deployer uses an AI system under its own authority (e.g. you publish AI-generated images). The obligations differ: providers handle interaction notices and output marking; deployers handle deepfake and public-interest-text disclosure. If you only use third-party tools like ChatGPT, you are usually a deployer.

Are there penalties for non-compliance?

The AI Act provides for administrative fines for breaches of the transparency obligations — up to EUR 15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher (Article 99), applied by national authorities. In practice the near-term risk for most SMEs is a complaint or enquiry plus reputational exposure. Putting clear disclosures and documentation in place is a low-cost way to show good faith.
